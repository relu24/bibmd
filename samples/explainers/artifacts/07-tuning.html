Harmonics & temperament

Why every piano is slightly out of tune

A freshly tuned piano is out of tune on purpose. Every interval on it except the octave is a little wrong, by an amount a tuner can count in beats per second. It is not a flaw of the instrument; it is arithmetic. Twelve keys cannot hold the ratios that make music sound sweet, and the story of why starts with a single vibrating string.

Listen for the slow shimmer in the second one. That is the subject of this page.

1 · The harmonic series

A string sings many notes at once

A string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in shapes that fit a whole number of half-waves between the ends. Mode n has n half-waves and vibrates n times as fast as the first: if the whole string sounds at f, the modes sound at f, 2f, 3f, 4f… This ladder is the harmonic series, and every pitched instrument sounds several rungs of it at once. 2 · Consonance

Two notes agree when their harmonics do

Add two waves of slightly different frequency and they drift in and out of step: in step they reinforce, out of step they cancel. The loudness swells and fades at the difference of the two frequencies. That is the identity sin a + sin b = 2 cos((a−b)/2) · sin((a+b)/2): a tone at the average pitch, inside an envelope that beats |f1 − f2| times a second.

Drawn slowed down (an 8 Hz wave instead of 440 Hz) so the cycles are visible; the beat rate is the real one, over two seconds.

Now two real notes, each with its ladder of harmonics. When their frequency ratio is a simple fraction like 3:2, harmonics of the two notes land exactly on top of each other (the 3rd of the lower on the 2nd of the upper) and nothing beats. Nudge the ratio off and those coinciding harmonics separate by a few hertz and begin to beat: the interval turns sour. Beats around 20–30 a second sound roughest. 3 · The Pythagorean comma

The circle of fifths does not close

Tune by pure fifths, the sweetest interval after the octave: start on C, go up a 3:2 fifth to G, another to D, and so on. Fold each note back into one octave. After twelve fifths you have visited every key and should be back on C. You are not. Twelve pure fifths are (3/2)12 ≈ 129.75; seven octaves are 27 = 128. The overshoot, 531441 : 524288, is the Pythagorean comma: about 23.5 cents, a quarter of a semitone. No powers of 3 will ever equal a power of 2, so the circle can never close.

4 · Equal temperament

Share the error, twelve ways

The modern solution is to give up on every pure interval except the octave. Divide the octave into twelve equal steps, each a ratio of

21/12 = 1.059463…

so that twelve of them make exactly 2. Each tempered fifth is 700 cents instead of 701.96, two cents narrow; twelve of them absorb the comma and the circle closes. Every key now sounds the same, and you can modulate anywhere. The price is that no interval but the octave is pure. Cents are the ruler: an interval of ratio r is 1200·log2 r cents, a tempered semitone is exactly 100. Click a row to see, and hear, what the difference does.

What you just saw

A compromise you can hear

A string sounds a whole ladder of harmonics. Two notes sound sweet when their ladders share rungs, which happens at simple ratios like 2:1, 3:2 and 5:4. But those ratios are built from powers of 2, 3 and 5, and no stack of them ever lands back on a pure octave: twelve fifths overshoot seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma. Equal temperament spreads that error evenly. Fifths and fourths come out two cents off, almost pure; major thirds are nearly fourteen cents sharp, and on a piano you can hear them shimmer about ten times a second around middle C.

Real pianos add one more twist: their stiff steel strings have harmonics that run slightly sharp of the ideal ladder, so tuners stretch the octaves a little wider than 2:1 to keep them beat-free. Even the octave, the one interval the system promised to keep, is bent. Every piano is slightly out of tune, and that is how it is in tune.
