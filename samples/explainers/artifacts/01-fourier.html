Every wave is a sum of circles

A circle spinning at a steady rate traces a sine wave. Hang a smaller, faster circle off its rim, and another off that one, and the tip can draw almost anything: a square wave with sharp corners, a sawtooth, a triangle. That is Fourier's idea, and it runs your phone's audio, your JPEGs and your radio.

01Stacking sines

Start with one sine wave, sin x. It is a poor square wave: round where the square is flat, smooth where it jumps. Now add sin 3x / 3, a wave three times as fast and a third as tall. The shoulders lift and the middle flattens. Keep adding odd harmonics, each 1/k as tall, and the sum hugs the square tighter and tighter.

The three shapes need different recipes. The square uses only odd harmonics at 1/k. The sawtooth uses every harmonic, with alternating signs. The triangle uses odd harmonics at 1/k², so they die away fast: a triangle has no jumps, only corners, and smooth things need little high-frequency help.

02Circles on circles

Every term b·sin(kx) is the height of a point on a circle of radius b turning k times while the first circle turns once. Put each circle's centre on the previous circle's rim and the tip's height is the sum. Ancient astronomers used the same trick, "epicycles", to fit planetary orbits.

03The overshoot that never goes away

Look closely at a jump. Next to it the sum overshoots, rings, and settles. Add more terms and the ringing squeezes closer to the jump, but its first peak does not shrink. With a hundred terms or a million, it rises about 9% of the jump above the top. This is the Gibbs phenomenon, first worked out by Henry Wilbraham in 1848 and later by J. Willard Gibbs.

Why? Each finite sum is smooth, and a smooth curve cannot jump. It must climb from −1 to +1 in a sliver of width about π/N, and squeezing a fixed shape into a thinner sliver keeps its height. The error shrinks in area, not in peak: the sum converges "on average", not at every point at once.

04Which harmonics are in it?

The list of amplitudes is the wave's spectrum: a recipe instead of a picture. Equalisers, MP3 and noise filters edit sound by editing this list. Click a bar to switch that harmonic off and on and watch what the wave loses.

harmonics on energy kept

Removing the fundamental leaves a wave with the same jumps but a wobbling top: the edges live in the high harmonics, the overall shape in the low ones. Keep only the first three and you have a low-pass filter: the edges blur but the beat remains.

What you just saw

  1. A periodic wave can be written as a sum of sines whose frequencies are whole multiples of one base frequency.
  2. Each sine is a spinning circle; chaining circles adds their heights, so the tip of the chain draws the wave.
  3. The size of each term tells you how rough the wave is: jumps give amplitudes falling like 1/k, corners like 1/k².
  4. At a jump, any finite sum overshoots by about 9% of the jump, however many terms you take (Gibbs).
  5. The list of amplitudes, the spectrum, is a complete description of the wave, and editing it is filtering.
