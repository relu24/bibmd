Population genetics, by hand

Chance and choice in evolution

A new mutation appears in one individual. It makes its carriers a little better at surviving: one percent more offspring, say. Will it spread through the species?

Usually, no. Most beneficial mutations vanish within a few generations, lost by plain bad luck before selection ever gets a grip. Evolution is a contest between choice (natural selection, which is systematic) and chance (genetic drift, which is not). This page builds both from the simplest possible model: one gene, two versions of it, A and a.

Selection is a nudge. Drift is a coin toss. Which wins depends on the size of the nudge and the size of the population.
ONE

No change without a cause: Hardy–Weinberg

Every individual carries two copies of the gene, one from each parent. If a fraction p of all copies in the population are A and q = 1 − p are a, and mates pair up at random, then an offspring draws its two copies independently: AA with chance p², Aa with 2pq (A from mother and a from father, or the other way round) and aa with q². Those three always add up to (p + q)² = 1.

The surprise is what happens next: nothing. The offspring pass on A at the same rate p, so the next generation has the same proportions, and the next. Without selection, drift, migration or mutation, allele frequencies stay put forever.

TWO

Choice: natural selection

Now let the genotypes differ in fitness: the average number of offspring an individual leaves, relative to the others. After selection, the share of A copies becomes

p′ = (p²·w_AA + pq·w_Aa) / W, W = p²·w_AA + 2pq·w_Aa + q²·w_aa

That is all of it: one line, applied generation after generation. Small differences in fitness compound like interest.

share of A, generation 0 → 200

Try A is recessive and helps: the same 5% advantage as the dominant case, but while A is rare it hides in heterozygotes, where it does nothing, so the curve crawls for a hundred generations before it takes off. Try heterozygote advantage: neither allele wins, and the population settles where p* = (w_Aa − w_aa) / (2w_Aa − w_AA − w_aa).

THREE

Chance: genetic drift

Selection treated the population as infinite. Real populations are finite: each generation is a sample of 2N gene copies drawn from the last, and samples wobble. Here are eight populations, all starting at 50% A, with no selection at all. Each generation draws its 2N copies at random (the Wright–Fisher model).

share of A in eight populations, generation 0 → 100 · gridlines at 0, 25, 50, 75, 100%

Each generation's binomial draw of 2N copies is approximated by a normal draw (Box–Muller from a seeded Park–Miller generator) with the binomial's mean and variance, clamped to 0 and 2N; a count that reaches 0 or 2N stays there. The same generator and seeds as the bib version of this page, so the same lines.

With five individuals, most lines hit 0% or 100% within a few dozen generations: one allele is gone for good, by chance alone. With five hundred they barely leave the middle. Drift eats variation at a rate of 1/(2N) per generation, so its strength is set by population size.

FOUR

The race: selection against drift

Back to the new mutation. It starts as a single copy, frequency 1/(2N). A neutral mutation fixes with exactly that chance: every copy in the population is equally likely to become the ancestor of all future copies. A beneficial one with advantage s fixes with the chance worked out by Motoo Kimura,

u = (1 − e^(−2s)) / (1 − e^(−4Ns))

which is about 2s once 4Ns is well above 1, regardless of N. A 1% advantage gives a 2% chance: the mutation is lost 98 times in 100. When 4Ns is below 1, u is about 1/(2N): selection is invisible and the mutation behaves as if neutral.

WHAT YOU JUST SAW

Chance and choice

  • Hardy–Weinberg: random mating turns allele shares into genotype shares p², 2pq, q², and on its own changes nothing.
  • Selection is deterministic: one line, p′ = (p²w_AA + pqw_Aa)/W, repeated. Dominance decides how fast; heterozygote advantage keeps both alleles.
  • Drift is sampling error, of size 1/(2N) per generation. Small populations lose variation fast, whatever its worth.
  • The race: a beneficial mutation fixes with chance about 2s, a neutral one with 1/(2N). Selection can only steer what drift lets survive, and only when 4Ns is well above 1.
