An explainer about counting

The same votes, four different winners

Ninety-nine percent of an election is the voters. The last percent is the rule that turns their ballots into one name, and it is not neutral. Below is one town of 100 voters in five blocs, each ranking five candidates. Count their ballots five reasonable ways and you can get five different mayors.

1 · The electorate

Five blocs, one ranked ballot each

Every voter in a bloc casts the same ranking, best first. Drag a slider to grow or shrink a bloc; untick a candidate to take them off the ballot (their name is then skipped on every ranking). The tiles at the top follow you down the page.

2 · Plurality and the runoff

Count first choices, then maybe count again

Plurality looks only at first choices: the biggest pile wins, majority or not. A two-round runoff keeps the two biggest piles and asks everyone again, so each ballot goes to whichever of the two finalists it ranks higher. A candidate with a majority in round one wins outright.

3 · Instant runoff

Eliminate the weakest, one at a time

Instant-runoff voting runs the runoff many times without asking again. Each round, the candidate with the fewest first choices is eliminated, and their ballots move to the next name still standing. Repeat until someone holds a majority. The two-round runoff throws away everyone but two at once; instant runoff does it gently, and the order of elimination matters.

4 · Borda count

Points for every place, not just first

The Borda count reads the whole ballot. With n candidates, a first place earns n−1 points, second n−2, down to zero for last. A candidate nobody loves but nobody hates (many second places) can outscore a polarising favourite.

5 · Condorcet

Everyone against everyone

The Marquis de Condorcet proposed the most demanding test: hold a head-to-head race between every pair. A candidate who beats each rival one-on-one is the Condorcet winner, and arguably the people's choice. The catch, which he found himself: majorities need not be transitive. A can beat B, B beat C, and C beat A, a rock-paper-scissors with no winner at all.

6 · The spoiler

A candidate who loses can still decide who wins

Load the spoiler town. Amber leads on first choices with 38, but Blue and Coral share a camp: 55 voters prefer Blue to Amber. Under plurality, Coral's 20 votes split that camp and Amber wins. Take Coral off the ballot, a candidate who was never going to win, and the winner changes.

In the first town every candidate wins under some rule, so any withdrawal is felt. Take Amber off the ballot (the runoff is the only rule it wins) and Dusk wins four rules out of five. Methods that read the whole ranking feel it too: Borda's points depend on how many candidates there are to rank.

What you just saw

There is no perfect rule

The voters never changed. Plurality rewards the biggest bloc, the runoff the strongest finalist, instant runoff the survivor of a particular elimination order, Borda the broadly acceptable, Condorcet the one who wins every duel, when such a candidate exists. Each is a defensible reading of “what the town wants”.

In 1951 Kenneth Arrow proved that this is not a failure of imagination. With three or more candidates, no rule that turns ranked ballots into a ranking of society can satisfy all of these at once: unanimity (if every voter prefers X to Y, so does the result), independence of irrelevant alternatives (whether X beats Y depends only on how voters rank X against Y, so no spoilers), and no dictator (the result is not simply one voter's ballot). Something has to give. Choosing a voting rule is choosing which flaw you will live with.
