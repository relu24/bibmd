Flattening the curve

One person catches a new virus. A few months later, either most of a city of a million has had it, or almost nobody has, and the hospitals were either overrun or quietly coped. The difference comes down to a handful of numbers you can move below. Every chart on this page is computed live from the same small model.

An epidemic is a chain reaction. Everything that matters is in how the chain grows, and in what makes it stop.
Part one

One person's chain

R₀ ("R nought") is the average number of people one infected person passes the virus to, in a population where nobody is immune yet. Each case below infects a random number of people with that average (a Poisson draw), and the chain is followed for four generations.

Part two

From chains to curves: the SIR model

A chain cannot grow forever: the people it reaches are increasingly people who have already had it. The SIR model splits a population into Susceptible, Infectious and Recovered (immune) and moves people between them each day:

S— β·S·I per day → I— I / D per day → R

where β = R₀ / D and D is how long someone stays infectious. The model starts with 100 infectious people in a city of a million, and steps forward one day at a time (Heun's method, a careful version of "rate × one day").

The epidemic peaks exactly when the susceptible share S falls below 1/R₀: from then on each case infects fewer than one new person, because most of the people it meets are already immune. A longer infectious period with the same R₀ does not make the epidemic bigger; it stretches it out.

Part three

Flattening the curve

Suppose 5% of infectious people need a hospital bed, and the city has 2,000 beds. At the peak above, that is far more than the hospitals have. Flattening buys time: the peak is lower and later, so the same beds can treat everyone. Notice what it does not do on its own: if the measures are lifted while most people are still susceptible (try Lasts = 60), the curve simply comes back. Contact reduction holds an epidemic down; only immunity ends it.

Part four

Herd immunity and the final size

If a share v of people are immune before the virus arrives (say, vaccinated), each case meets fewer susceptible people, and the effective reproduction number is R = R₀·(1 − v). The epidemic cannot take off when that is below one, that is when

v ≥ 1 − 1/R₀, the herd immunity threshold.

And when it does take off, how many people does it reach in the end? The SIR model answers without simulating: the share z of the population ever infected solves the final-size relation z = s₀·(1 − e−R₀·z), where s₀ = 1 − v is the share that was susceptible to begin with.

Two things are worth seeing here. First, the red curve is always above the orange one: an unchecked epidemic does not stop at the herd immunity threshold, it overshoots it, because at the peak there are still many infectious people and each infects nearly one more on the way down. At R₀ = 2.5 the threshold is 60% but the epidemic reaches about 89%. Second, drag v up to the threshold and the red curve drops to zero at your R₀: immunity given in advance prevents the overshoot entirely.

What you just saw

  • R₀ is an average of a random process. Chains above R₀ = 1 tend to explode, but a single introduction can still fizzle out by luck.
  • The SIR model turns chains into curves. The epidemic peaks when the susceptible share falls to 1/R₀; the infectious period sets the pace, not the size.
  • Flattening lowers and delays the peak so the hospitals cope. Lifted too early, it only postpones the wave.
  • Herd immunity at 1 − 1/R₀ is the point where the epidemic turns, not where it ends: left alone it overshoots to the final size z = s₀(1 − e−R₀z). Immunity in advance, above the threshold, means no wave at all.

The model is deliberately simple: everyone mixes with everyone, immunity lasts, and nobody is born or moves. Real epidemics add age, places, seasons and chance, but this skeleton is what they are built on.
