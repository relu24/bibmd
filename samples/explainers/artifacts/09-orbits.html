Orbital mechanics, by hand

To catch up, slow down

Two spacecraft share an orbit. The one behind wants to catch the one ahead. On a road you would press the accelerator. In orbit, that sends you backwards: the push lifts you into a higher, slower orbit and the gap grows. To catch up you brake.

This page builds that strange rule from one equation, then uses it to fly to Mars. Every number is computed live from the sliders; nothing is a picture.

In orbit, speed and height are one thing. Change one and you have changed the other.
ONE

Newton's cannon

Put a cannon on a tower 6,371 km above the ground, twice the planet's radius from its centre, and fire sideways. Too slow and the shot falls in. At one exact speed it falls around the planet forever: a circle. Faster still and the circle stretches into an ellipse, until at √2 times the circular speed it never comes back.

The orbit follows from just the position and the velocity at launch. With the sideways launch here the eccentricity is simply e = |v²/v_c² − 1|.

The dot moves by time, not by angle: press fly and it lingers far out and whips round close in. The speed comes from one line, the vis-viva equation:

v² = GM (2/r − 1/a)

Here r is the distance from the planet's centre now and a the semi-major axis, half the long width of the ellipse. A circle is the case r = a at every point, so v² = GM/r. The curve below is that speed over one orbit.

TWO

Lower is faster

For a circle, v = √(GM/r): the closer in, the faster. And the period grows as r^1.5 (Kepler's third law), so a low orbit is both faster and shorter. Four circular orbits of the same planet:

Now the chase. Two craft fly a circular orbit 400 km up; the chaser is behind. It fires its engine once, along or against its motion. Fire forward and the extra speed lifts the far side of its orbit: a bigger ellipse, a longer period, and it falls further behind on every lap. Fire backward and it drops into a smaller, quicker ellipse and gains on every lap.

Braking lowered the chaser's orbit, and the lower orbit is the faster one. To actually dock it would then burn forward to climb back up, once it has caught up.

THREE

The Hohmann transfer to Mars

The same trick, on the scale of the Sun. Earth circles at 1 AU, Mars at 1.524 AU. The cheapest two-burn route is an ellipse that just touches both orbits: leave Earth's orbit with a forward burn at the ellipse's closest point to the Sun, coast half an ellipse, and burn forward again at Mars to match its orbit. (These are speeds relative to each planet's orbit; escaping Earth's own gravity is a separate cost.)

Look at the arrival: the craft reaches Mars's orbit slower than Mars, because it has climbed uphill against the Sun the whole way. The second burn is Mars catching up and the craft speeding up to match.

FOUR

The launch window

The transfer takes 259 days, and in that time Mars moves 136° round its orbit. So Mars must be 44° ahead of Earth on launch day, or the craft arrives at an empty point in space. Earth laps Mars every 780 days (the synodic period), so the angle comes round again only then. Slide the launch date and watch the angle drift.

WHAT YOU JUST SAW

The one idea underneath

  • Position and velocity fix the orbit. One speed gives a circle, more an ellipse, √2 times as much an escape. The shot slows as it climbs and speeds up as it falls.
  • Vis-viva, v² = GM(2/r − 1/a), ties speed to height and to the orbit's size.
  • Lower is faster, so a brake makes you gain and a push makes you lose. Rendezvous works backwards from intuition.
  • Hohmann: two forward burns, 2.95 and 2.65 km/s, half an ellipse, 259 days, and a launch only when Mars is 44° ahead, which happens once every 780 days.
