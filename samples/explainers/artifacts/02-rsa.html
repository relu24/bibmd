A lock anyone can close but only you can open

Imagine handing out open padlocks to the whole world. Anyone can snap one shut on a box and send it to you; only your key opens it. That is public-key cryptography, and RSA builds the padlock out of nothing but two prime numbers and remainders.

01Two primes make a key

Pick two primes, p and q, and keep them secret. Their product n = p·q goes public. So does a small number e, the "closing" exponent. The private "opening" exponent d is the number that undoes e, and computing it needs φ = (p−1)(q−1), which you can only get if you know p and q.

p and q must be different primes: with p = q, anyone can take a square root of n.

Finding d: the extended Euclidean algorithm

We need d with e·d ≡ 1 (mod φ). Euclid's algorithm divides repeatedly, keeping remainders; the extended version also carries a coefficient t so that every remainder equals t·e modulo φ. When the remainder reaches 1, its t is the inverse.

02Closing the lock

To encrypt, turn each letter into a number m (here its character code: A = 65 … Z = 90, space = 32) and compute c = me mod n. Anyone can do it: it uses only the public n and e. To decrypt, compute m = cd mod n. Click a letter to see the arithmetic.

Square and multiply

Computing me directly would produce enormous numbers; instead, write the exponent in binary and walk its bits from the left. For each bit, square the running value; if the bit is 1, also multiply by m. Reduce modulo n after every step, so nothing ever exceeds n². Fewer than 2·(number of bits) multiplications, even for exponents with hundreds of digits.

03Why nobody else can open it

An attacker sees n and e. To get d they need φ, and to get φ they need p and q: they must factor n. For our tiny n that is instant. The simplest attack is trial division: try 2, 3, 5, 7, … up to √n.

But √n has half as many digits as n, so every two extra digits of n make trial division ten times longer. Multiplying p·q takes about digits² steps; undoing it takes about 10digits/2. Slide the size of n:

Cleverer methods exist. The best known, the number field sieve, grows more slowly than trial division, but still faster than any power of the number of digits: the largest RSA challenge number factored in public had 250 digits and took about 2,700 core-years of computing. Doubling the digits does not double the attacker's work; it multiplies it beyond reach.

What you just saw

  1. RSA's public key is a product n = p·q and an exponent e; the private key d is the inverse of e modulo (p−1)(q−1), found with the extended Euclidean algorithm.
  2. Encrypting is me mod n, decrypting is cd mod n; because e·d = 1 + k·φ, raising to e then d lands back on m (Euler's theorem).
  3. Square-and-multiply makes huge powers cheap, and reducing mod n at each step keeps every number small.
  4. Security rests on an asymmetry: multiplying two primes is easy, factoring their product is not.
  5. Caveat: encrypting letter by letter, as here, is insecure (equal letters give equal ciphers). Real RSA uses 2048-bit or larger n and pads each message with randomness first.
