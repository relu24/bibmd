Special relativity · an interactive explainer

Moving clocks run slow

Not because their gears get tired, and not as an illusion. It falls straight out of one stubborn fact: every observer measures the same speed of light. All you need is a triangle.

High in the atmosphere, cosmic rays make muons: particles that live, on average, 2.2 millionths of a second. Even at the speed of light that is only 660 metres of travel. They are born 15 km up. Yet detectors on the ground catch them by the thousand. By the end of this page you will know why.
01 — THE LIGHT CLOCK

A clock made of light

Take the simplest clock imaginable: a flash of light bouncing between two mirrors a distance L apart. Each trip from one mirror to the other is one tick, and takes L/c. Now put an identical clock on a ship moving to the right at speed v. Watch both from the ground.

From the ground, the moving photon cannot go straight up: the top mirror has moved on by the time the light arrives, so the light runs along a diagonal. A diagonal is longer than the vertical. And light, for everybody, goes at exactly c. Longer path, same speed: each tick of the moving clock takes longer. The ship's clock runs slow.

02 — THE TRIANGLE

Pythagoras finds γ

Freeze one tick of the moving clock. In the time t the ground measures, the light covers the hypotenuse c·t while the ship moves v·t sideways. The vertical leg is still the mirror gap, L = c·t₀, where t₀ is a tick on a clock at rest.

  1. Pythagoras: (c·t)² = (c·t₀)² + (v·t)²
  2. Collect the t: t²(c² − v²) = c²·t₀²
  3. Divide by c²: t²(1 − v²/c²) = t₀²
  4. Take the root:
t = γ · t₀,   γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)

γ, the Lorentz factor, is the stretch. Here it is against speed. Click or drag on the curve. It barely leaves 1 at everyday speeds (a jet at 250 m/s has γ − 1 ≈ 3·10⁻¹³) and then shoots to infinity as v approaches c.

The same γ shrinks lengths along the motion: a moving ruler is 1/γ as long as at rest (look at the moving clock above: it is squeezed). Time stretches by γ, length shrinks by γ, and the two together keep the speed of light the same for everyone.

Two stories, one answer. On Earth: the traveller's clock runs slow by γ, so she ages 2d / (v·γ). On the ship: nothing is wrong with her clock, but the trip is shorter: the distance to the star is length-contracted to d/γ. Both give the same age.

Why isn't it symmetric, each twin seeing the other's clock as slow? Because only the traveller turns around. The kink in her worldline is a real, felt acceleration, and it is the stay-at-home twin whose path through spacetime is the long one between the two meetings.

04 — THE MUONS

Back to the sky

A muon's mean life at rest is τ = 2.197 μs. Out of a million made at altitude h, the number still alive after flying for a time t is 1,000,000 · e−t/τ. The question is just: whose t?

Muons still alive (of 1,000,000) as they fall from the birth altitude to the ground. Grey: if their clocks ran at the ground's rate. Orange: with time dilation.

From the ground, the muon's internal clock is slowed by γ, so it has γ lifetimes to spend on the way down. From the muon's side its clock is fine, but the atmosphere rushes at it contracted to h/γ, a few hundred metres. Same survivors either way. This was measured: in the 1940s and, precisely, in 1963 on a mountain and at sea level.

WHAT YOU JUST SAW
  • A light clock in motion traces a diagonal; with light's speed fixed, the tick gets longer.
  • Pythagoras on that diagonal gives γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²): about 1 for anything we build, unbounded near c.
  • Lengths along the motion shrink by the same γ, so each observer's account agrees on what actually happens.
  • The twin who turns around ages less: a trip of 2·10 ly at 0.9 c costs her 9.7 years, not 22.2.
  • Cosmic-ray muons reach the ground because their clocks, seen from here, run γ times slow.
Units: c = 1 light-year per year in the trip calculator; c = 299,792 km/s for the muons.
