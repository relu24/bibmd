How a machine learns: rolling downhill

Strip away the jargon and most of machine learning is one move, repeated: measure how wrong you are, work out which way is downhill, take a small step. Here is that move, on a problem small enough to see whole.

Twelve points, one straight line. You will fit it by hand, then watch a machine fit it by rolling down a landscape of its own mistakes, and then see the two ways that rolling goes wrong.
1 A GUESS AND ITS ERROR

Fit a line by hand

The model is a line, y = w·x + b: a slope w and an intercept b. Move them. The thin vertical strokes are the errors, how far each point is from the line. The machine's whole notion of "wrong" is the average of their squares, the mean squared error.

loss(w, b) = (1/12) · Σ (w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)²

2 THE LANDSCAPE

Every guess is a place on a map

Each pair (w, b) has a loss. Draw all of them and you get a terrain: across is slope, up is intercept, colour is height. Your guess is the dot (click the map to move it). The best line sits at the bottom of the long, narrow valley. The arrow is the negative gradient: the direction in which the loss falls fastest, worked out from the data by calculus,

∂loss/∂w = (2/12)·Σ (w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)·xᵢ    ∂loss/∂b = (2/12)·Σ (w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)
Bands are contours of equal loss; each band is about 1.65× higher than the one inside it. The valley is narrow because the slope matters far more than the intercept: x runs up to 5, so a change in w moves the line much more than the same change in b.

3 ROLLING DOWNHILL

Gradient descent

From your guess, step against the gradient, scaled by a learning rate η, and repeat:

w ← w − η·∂loss/∂w     b ← b − η·∂loss/∂b

Try η = 0.005 (it crawls), 0.05 (steady), 0.088 (it zig-zags across the valley but gets there) and 0.095 (each zig is bigger than the last: it flies off the map). For this data the edge is η = 2/λ = 0.0923, where λ = 21.66 is the curvature across the valley; past it, descent diverges.

4 BUMPS

Stuck in a dip, and how momentum helps

The line-fitting loss is a perfect bowl, with one bottom. Real models have landscapes full of bumps. Here is a one-dimensional one, f(x) = 0.08x² + 0.55·sin(2.2x). Plain descent stops in the first dip it reaches: a local minimum. Momentum lets the ball keep a share β of its previous velocity, so it can roll through a small dip and over the next rise:

v ← β·v − η·f′(x)     x ← x + v

Momentum is not magic: from some starts it overshoots the best valley too, and too much of it oscillates for ages. Every serious optimiser (with names like Adam) is a refinement of these two dials: how big a step, and how much to remember.

WHAT YOU JUST SAW
  • "Learning" here is choosing two numbers, w and b, to make one number, the loss, small.
  • The loss over all choices is a landscape; its gradient points uphill, so the machine steps the other way.
  • The learning rate is the stride: too small crawls, a bit large zig-zags, past 2/(steepest curvature) it diverges.
  • Bumpy landscapes trap plain descent in local minima; momentum carries it over small rises.
  • A network with a billion weights does exactly this, in a billion dimensions.
