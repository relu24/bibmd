The test is 99% accurate. You tested positive. Should you worry?

A screening test for a rare condition comes back positive. The leaflet says the test is 99% accurate: it catches 99 of every 100 people who have the condition, and clears 99 of every 100 who don't. So what is the chance you actually have it?

Most people, and in surveys most doctors, say about 99%. Make your own guess before reading on.

A coin flip. Not because the test is bad, but because the condition is rare. The rest of this page shows why, with a thousand people you can count.

Part two

The natural-frequency tree

The same thousand people, split twice: first by whether they are sick, then by what the test says. Only the two "test positive" branches matter to someone holding a positive result.

P(sick | positive) = sick positives / all positives

This is all of Bayes' theorem: a probability after the evidence is the share of the people with that evidence who are sick. Counting people instead of multiplying percentages is the trick: researchers who give doctors the question as natural frequencies ("10 out of 1,000") get far more right answers than with percentages.

Part three

Test again

You have one positive result, and the chance of being sick that Part two worked out (50% at the starting settings). A second, independent test starts from there: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. Add results one at a time and watch the probability move.

Each positive multiplies the odds of being sick by the same factor, the likelihood ratio sensitivity / (1 − specificity), which is 99 for a 99/99 test. One positive takes 1 : 99 odds to about 1 : 1; a second takes 1 : 1 to about 99 : 1. This is why a positive screening result is followed by a different, confirmatory test rather than a diagnosis. (It only works if the two tests' errors are independent: the same test repeated on a person whose body fools it will be fooled twice.)

Part four

It depends on who is tested

The same test means different things in different crowds. Screening everyone, the prevalence is low and positives are often false; testing people with symptoms, the prevalence is high and a positive is convincing. Drag the specificity up from 99% to 99.9% and watch the red curve shift left by a factor of ten: for screening a rare condition, the false-positive rate matters far more than catching every case. A positive is a coin flip exactly where the prevalence equals (1 − specificity) / (sensitivity + 1 − specificity), about 1% for a 99/99 test.

What you just saw

  • Accuracy is not the answer. "99% accurate" describes the test; the chance that you are sick also depends on how common the condition is among people like you.
  • Count people, not percentages. Of 1,000 people with a 1% condition, about 10 are sick and test positive, and about 10 healthy people test positive too. Half the positives are false.
  • Bayes' theorem is that count written as a formula: P(sick | +) = sick positives / all positives.
  • Evidence accumulates. The posterior after one test is the prior for the next; two independent positives make it close to certain, and one negative undoes a lot.
  • Who is tested matters as much as the test. Low prevalence plus imperfect specificity means many false alarms.
